# Best $10 I've ever spent on an LLM (and my dog wrote it)

> *"Once upon a time, there was a dog named Cookie. She loved to play fetch with her owner. One day, they went for a walk in the park and found a big ball. Cookie picked it up with her mouth and brought it back to his owner..."*

That paragraph above? Generated by a 91-million-parameter transformer I trained from scratch over a Saturday afternoon, on a corpus of synthetic children's stories. The dog Cookie is real. She's mine. She is currently asleep on the couch, blissfully unaware that there's now a small language model wandering the internet that can write fan-fiction about her.

Total compute cost: about **\$5** of rented H100 time across three training runs. The headline is generous; the actual final run cost \$1.50. I'm calling it \$10 because that's the round number I'd be willing to spend again, and because "$1.50" makes a less catchy post.

What this is is **a hands-on log of training a language model end-to-end**, from "I have heard of `model.generate()`" to "my custom transformer architecture writes coherent short stories about my pet." I'll go through:

1. What I built (and what it actually means at this scale)
2. The journey from gibberish to coherence — three training runs, what worked, what didn't
3. The bugs that ate hours
4. The mental models I now have that I didn't have last week
5. What the next step looks like

This is the long version. The LinkedIn one will be ~3 paragraphs. Bear with me.

---

## What I built

A 91M-parameter GPT-style transformer, trained from scratch on the [TinyStories corpus](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories) — about 470 million tokens of synthetic children's stories generated by GPT-3.5 / GPT-4 using a deliberately-constrained vocabulary (~1,500 base words, the kind a 3–4 year old understands).

Architecture (borrowed wholesale from Andrej Karpathy's [nanochat](https://github.com/karpathy/nanochat)):

| Component | Choice | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Causal multi-head, RoPE positional encoding | Lets each token attend to all earlier tokens; RoPE encodes position into the rotation of Q/K vectors |
| Normalisation | RMSNorm (no learnable scale) | Keeps activations stable across layers |
| Activation | ReLU² in MLP | Squared ReLU; cheaper than GELU, similar quality at this scale |
| Tokenizer | 4,096-vocab byte-level BPE, custom-trained on TinyStories | Maps text → integer IDs |
| Stack | 12 layers × 768 hidden dim × 12 heads × 64 head dim | Standard "GPT-2-base shape" |
| Context | 512 tokens | Enough for ~99% of TinyStories' story lengths |

Implemented with the **Hugging Face stack** — `transformers`, `tokenizers`, `datasets`, `safetensors`. Same APIs that power production training pipelines, just at the smallest possible scale where they still work.

For perspective on scale:

| Model | Params | Training tokens | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-2 base (2019) | 124M | ~10B | (Microsoft Azure money) |
| Karpathy's nanochat speedrun | 561M | ~12B | ~$100 |
| **My d12 model** | **91M** | **~830M** | **~$1.50** |
| GPT-4-class | ~1.7T | ~13T | $hundreds-of-millions |

So this is roughly 1/100,000th the scale of GPT-4. But — and this matters — the *pipeline shape is identical*. Same tokenizer, same architecture family, same loss function, same training loop, same checkpoint format. Going from this to a 7B model is bigger numbers, more compute, more engineering polish — not a different shape of project.

## The journey: from "torch.Size errors" to actual stories

### Round 1: Smoke-test on my laptop (gibberish, expected)

The first training run was a 28M-param model on Wikipedia text (WikiText-2, ~2 million tokens), 500 steps, on Apple Silicon MPS. Took ~3 minutes.

Final perplexity: **194**. That means at every position, the model's probability distribution was as confused as if it were uniformly choosing between 194 equally-likely next tokens. (For reference: a totally untrained model with our 4,096-vocab tokenizer has perplexity 4,096.)

The model could *barely* tell that English is more likely than random — but it couldn't string two words together coherently. Output was unreadable token soup.

This run was always going to produce gibberish. The point was just to confirm the pipeline ran end-to-end without crashing. **It did.** Loss went down. `save_pretrained()` produced a folder. `from_pretrained()` loaded it back. Pipeline confirmed.

But: **this told me three things I'd been wrong about:**

1. **Wikipedia is the wrong corpus for tiny models.** Wikipedia has world knowledge, named entities, dates, technical jargon. A 28M model has nowhere near the capacity to fit that distribution. You can't learn "Napoleon" if you've only seen it twice in 500 training steps and your model has 4,096 ways to be wrong about it.

2. **Apple Silicon MPS is real but quirky.** The training worked but threw a few NaN errors during sampling that turned out to be PyTorch-MPS-kernel-level bugs (more on this below).

3. **The architecture was fine.** Same code that produced gibberish here would, with a different corpus and more steps, produce coherent stories.

### Round 2: The actual first attempt (15K steps, RunPod H100, ~$0.30)

Time to switch to a real GPU. I went with [RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/) because their signup is the lowest-friction of the rented-GPU providers (Lambda Labs, GCP, AWS all require quota approvals or jump-through-hoops). $10 in credits, an SSH key uploaded, an H100 SXM 80GB pod deployed in 2 minutes.

Switched the corpus to TinyStories (~470M tokens), bumped the model to 28M params (8 layers × 512 hidden), trained for 15,000 steps with bf16 autocast and `torch.compile`.

```
Step :     0/15000  | val: 8.40 | ppl: 4054 | lr: 1.50e-06
Step :  1000/15000  | val: 2.16 | ppl:    8.6
Step :  5000/15000  | val: 1.61 | ppl:    5.0
Step : 14999/15000  | val: 1.40 | ppl:    4.0  | 1.4M tok/s
```

**6 minutes, $0.30, perplexity 4.** From "literally random" to "decent prediction" in 6 minutes of GPU time.

Then I generated text:

> *"Once upon a time, there was a dog named Cookie.. Igaintto-doo. He is like it was gone. It was a very sad. 'Iwifted and happy toreatenna. Once upon a bit. Once upon a little Annabutterram and a little girlsmagulled..."*

I burst out laughing. The model *almost* speaks English. Sentence-shaped phrases ("It was a very sad", "He is like it was gone") interleaved with confident-sounding nonsense ("Igaintto-doo", "Annabutterram", "girlsmagulled"). Imagine a toddler who has read a lot of children's books but doesn't yet know what most words mean — that's roughly what this model is.

Worse, every other prompt collapsed into a loop:

> *"Once upon a lotion. Once upon a lotion. Once upon a lotion appeared on the little girlsing the other kids..."*

This is **mode collapse**. The sampler had picked a high-probability path ("Once upon a") and got stuck reusing it because nothing in the temperature/top-k regime was pushing it to diverge.

So: model trained correctly (loss curve was clean, monotonically decreasing). Output was unusable. Two separate problems: the model itself was undertrained, and the sampling was uncontrolled. Both fixable.

### Diagnostic: was the model itself the bottleneck, or just the training?

This is the moment I learned my favorite mental model: **signal 1 — did the loss curve flatten?**

The idea: at the end of any training run, look at the last 25% of the val loss curve. How much did it drop in that window? If it's still meaningfully decreasing, you stopped early — more training would help. If it's basically flat, you've hit the model's capacity ceiling.

For my 15K run, the last 25% of training (steps 11,250 → 15,000) dropped val loss from ~1.5 to ~1.4 — about a **7% drop**. Way more than the "<1% means flattened" threshold. So the model wasn't done; it had headroom.

Combined with two other signals:

- **Chinchilla-optimal compute** (Hoffmann et al. 2022) says you want roughly 20 tokens per parameter. My run saw 250M tokens for 28M params = ~9× ratio. Way under the recommended ~20×. **Definitely undertrained.**
- **Published TinyStories floor**: the [original TinyStories paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759) showed 28M models can hit perplexity ~2.5 on this corpus. I was at 4.0. **Significant headroom.**

Three independent signals, all pointing at "this model could be much better — train more."

### Round 3: 50K steps (~$1, ~20 minutes)

Same architecture, same corpus, just bumped `NUM_STEPS = 15_000` to `NUM_STEPS = 50_000`. Also fixed sampling: added `repetition_penalty=1.3` and `top_p=0.95` (nucleus sampling) to break the mode collapse.

```
Step :  5000/50000  | val: 1.61 | ppl:    5.0   ← where 15K run had ended
Step : 25000/50000  | val: 1.34 | ppl:    3.83
Step : 49999/50000  | val: 1.29 | ppl:    3.62
```

Final val perplexity: **3.62**, vs the 15K run's 4.0. About 9% improvement on the metric.

Last-25% drop: **0.29%**. Curve flatlined. The 50K run had hit the model's ceiling.

In sample quality, this looked like:

> *"Once upon a time, there was a dog named Cookie. Heiddy and her name, who had been so much toddlover. One day afterwards. John and she would always said hello in the little Jack decided hearing them both of course that they were very happy..."*

Real names (John, Jack, Mia, Max, Anna — all common in TinyStories). Real grammatical structures. The "Once upon a lotion" loop was gone, killed by the repetition penalty.

But still — **made-up words**: "toddlover", "histelling", "heabengaz", "youthough". Sentences locally OK, paragraphs incoherent. The model could plan one sentence ahead but not three.

That's not a sampling issue. That's the **capacity ceiling** of a 28M-param model. To get past it you need more parameters, not more training.

### Round 4: 91M params (the d12 run, ~$1.50, ~20 minutes)

Bumped the architecture: 12 layers (vs 8), 768 hidden dim (vs 512), 12 attention heads (vs 8). Total parameters: 28M → 91M. Same corpus, fewer steps (30K, since each step is ~4× more compute).

```
Step :     0/30000  | val: 8.40 | ppl: 4443
Step :  1000/30000  | val: 2.26 | ppl:    9.6
Step : 30000/30000  | val: ~1.0 | ppl:   ~2.7
```

Final perplexity: **~2.7** — within striking distance of the published TinyStories floor (~2.5).

And the output:

> *"Once upon a time, there was a dog named Cookie. She loved to play fetch with her owner. One day, they went for a walk in the park and found a big ball. Cookie picked it up with her mouth and brought it back to his owner.*
>
> *His owner threw the ball and Cookie ran after it as fast as she could. But when she came back, she had lost the ball! Her owner looked everywhere but couldn't find it.*
>
> *Cookie felt bad that he lost the ball so badly. He promised himself he would try again next time. From then on, everytime they went for walks, Cookie would always look at the ground where he last saw the ball. It made him happy because he knew how much fun playing fetch.*
>
> *The end."*

Look at what changed:

- **Character continuity**: Cookie persists across paragraphs. The model tracks who's doing what.
- **Causal chains**: "owner threw the ball" → "Cookie ran after it" → "she had lost the ball" → "everywhere but couldn't find it" → "felt bad" → "promised himself".
- **Story arc**: introduction, complication, resolution.
- **Ends naturally**: the model knows when stories end. It writes "The end." and starts a new one.
- **Pronoun confusion**: "her mouth and brought it back to his owner" — Cookie is sometimes "she" and sometimes "he". The model hasn't fully nailed pronoun consistency. This is the kind of thing GPT-2 base also struggles with, and it gets fixed by scale (more params + more data).

That third bullet — story arc — is the qualitative jump. The 28M model could produce locally-grammatical sentences. The 91M model produces **stories**. Same architecture family, same corpus; just more capacity. Capacity buys narrative tracking.

## The bugs that ate hours

In rough order of "how surprised I was":

### 1. The RoPE buffer NaN bug (lost ~30 min)

Inference on the d12 model crashed with:

```
RuntimeError: probability tensor contains either `inf`, `nan` or element < 0
```

…right at the `torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1)` step. Logits had NaN, softmax → NaN, multinomial errors.

I assumed it was an MPS issue (since I'd seen others). Switched to CPU. Same error. That was confusing — CPU shouldn't produce NaN spontaneously.

Diagnostic showed all parameter weights were fine (fp32, all finite). But: **the RoPE precomputed cos/sin buffers had 3 NaN entries out of 16,384**. Hugging Face's `from_pretrained` loading path uses an `init_empty_weights()` context for fast loading; that allocates tensors as uninitialised memory. For tensors that get filled from the saved checkpoint, no problem. But our cos/sin buffers were registered with `persistent=False` (so they're regenerated each time, not saved/loaded), and `_init_rope` apparently wasn't running cleanly inside HF's loading context. A few entries got missed.

3 NaN entries in 16,384 sounds insignificant. It isn't — every attention layer multiplies q and k by these cos/sin values via RoPE. One NaN entry poisons that whole token position. By the time you've gone through 12 attention layers, NaN has spread everywhere.

**Fix:** call `model._init_rope(seq_len, head_dim)` manually right after `from_pretrained`. Two lines. Took 5 minutes to add once we'd identified the bug.

**Why d8 (28M) "worked" with the same code path:** both runs had ~3 NaN entries, but the d8 model just got lucky — its NaN positions never landed on a token a sample actually visited during the prompt-and-completion lengths I was using. It's a stochastic bug, not a deterministic one.

### 2. MPS scaled_dot_product_attention NaN (lost ~15 min)

PyTorch's `F.scaled_dot_product_attention(..., is_causal=True)` on Apple's MPS backend produces NaN under certain conditions. It's a known issue.

**Fix:** wrap inference in `with sdpa_kernel(SDPBackend.MATH):` to force the unoptimised math implementation. About 5% slower; 100% reliable.

### 3. The scp footgun (lost ~20 min, plus laughter)

Ran:

```bash
scp -P 30948 -r root@.../checkpoints/final runs/h100_tinystories/checkpoints/final
```

`scp` interpretation when destination directory exists: "copy *into* it." Result: the new `final/` got copied *inside* the old `final/`, giving me a `final/final/final/...` Russian-doll structure. Spent 10 minutes confused about why my brand-new model had the wrong file timestamps.

**Lesson:** if `dst/` exists, scp creates `dst/src_basename/` whether you wanted that or not. Either `rm -rf dst/` first, or use trailing-slash semantics: `scp src/* dst/`.

### 4. Tokenizing the corpus inside the training script (cost ~15 min per restart)

First training script tokenized the entire 1.8 GB train.txt at startup via `tokenizer.encode(huge_string)`. Single-threaded, ~10 minutes. Worse: peaked memory usage past 8 GB during encode → got OOM-killed by the container watchdog mid-run.

**Fix:** move tokenization into `prepare_data.py`, do it once with `encode_batch` (parallel + lower peak memory), save to `train_ids.pt` / `val_ids.pt` via `torch.save`. Training startup went from 10 minutes to ~3 seconds.

### 5. `~/.cache/hf_pipeline` vs `runs/.../data_cache/` confusion

Initial copywork code used `~/.cache/hf_pipeline/` for everything — shared across all the staged scripts. When I started a real training run, I needed to keep that cache (used by my copywork practice files) separate from the run-specific cache. Moving paths to `runs/h100_tinystories/data_cache/` made experiments isolatable.

### 6. RunPod's SSH proxy doesn't support port forwarding

Tried to view live TensorBoard on the pod via `ssh -L 6006:localhost:6006 ...@ssh.runpod.io`. Got `unknown channel type: unsupported channel type` — RunPod's `ssh.runpod.io` proxy is hardened against tunneling.

**Fix:** use the "exposed TCP" connection variant (`ssh root@<ip> -p <port>`) which goes direct to the pod's sshd. Or — what I ended up doing — let training finish, scp `tb_logs/` back to laptop, run TensorBoard locally.

### 7. RunPod's auto-created network volume keeps billing after pod terminate

If you select "Persistent storage: Network volume" during pod deploy (default if you bump the volume size), RunPod creates a 50GB volume with a name like `nanochat-train-<random>`. Terminating the pod **does not** delete the volume. It bills $5/month forever until you find it in **Storage** and delete it manually.

Set a calendar reminder. Or just do `Pods → Terminate; Storage → Delete` as a single mental ritual.

## Mental models I picked up

These are the things I knew *of* before but didn't *internalise* until I'd trained a model with my own hands.

### Bits per token (BPT)

The actual unit of model quality. `BPT = log2(perplexity)`. The intuition:

- BPT 12 = totally random for our 4,096-vocab tokenizer
- BPT 2 = "model has narrowed the next token to ~4 likely options"
- BPT 1.4 = TinyStories floor for a transformer of any size
- BPT 0.6 = big-LLM territory on real-world text
- BPT 0 = perfect prediction

My d8 model: BPT 1.86. My d12 model: BPT ~1.4. The difference (~0.46 bits) means the d12 model's next-token distribution is `2^0.46 ≈ 1.4×` tighter on average. That's the difference between "writes locally-grammatical fragments" and "writes coherent stories."

**Why papers prefer BPT to perplexity:** linear comparisons (a 0.1 BPT difference is the same amount of information regardless of starting point) and direct interpretation as compressed file size (BPT × token_count / 8 = compressed bytes).

### Chinchilla optimality

For a fixed compute budget, the optimal model has roughly **20 training tokens per parameter**. Above that you've over-trained for the model size and would do better with a bigger model. Below it you've under-trained.

| Run | Params | Tokens trained | Ratio | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15K-step | 28M | 250M | **9×** | undertrained |
| 50K-step | 28M | 833M | 30× | slightly over (TinyStories floor anyway) |
| d12 (30K) | 91M | 983M | 11× | undertrained relative to Chinchilla, but TinyStories caps out |

Chinchilla ratio is a quick "do the math" check before committing to a training run. For our scale, with TinyStories' fixed corpus size, you can't easily hit 20× without re-using data multiple times — and once you exceed ~3 epochs you're memorising rather than learning.

### Capacity ceiling vs. corpus floor

These are two different floors that get confused:

- **Capacity ceiling**: the lowest loss *this specific architecture* can achieve on this corpus, given infinite training. Set by `n_layer` × `n_embd`. Going below requires a bigger model.
- **Corpus floor**: the lowest loss *any model* can achieve on this corpus. Set by the inherent entropy of the text. Going below it requires either richer text or a fundamentally smarter architecture.

For my d8 model: capacity ceiling ≈ perplexity 3.6. For d12: capacity ceiling ≈ perplexity 2.7. Corpus floor for TinyStories: ≈ 2.4. The corpus floor is approached asymptotically by ever-bigger models with diminishing returns.

Once you understand this distinction, scaling decisions become trivial:

- "Is loss still dropping at end of training?" → train longer, you haven't hit the **capacity ceiling**.
- "Loss flat, but quality not good enough?" → you're at the **capacity ceiling**. Bigger model.
- "Bigger model not helping?" → you're at the **corpus floor**. Different corpus.

### Why the tokenizer matters more than you think

I almost reused GPT-2's pretrained tokenizer (vocab 50,257). Stopped myself when I realised what it would mean for parameter allocation:

| Tokenizer | Vocab | Embedding params for n_embd=512 | % of 28M model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Custom (TinyStories-trained)** | **4,096** | **2.1M** | **~7%** |
| GPT-2 | 50,257 | 25.7M | ~92% |
| GPT-4o | 200,000 | 102M | ~365% (bigger than the model) |

If I'd used GPT-2's tokenizer, **65% of my model's "28M parameters" would be embedding rows for tokens that never appear in TinyStories**. Those rows would never receive a gradient. Effectively, my model would have ~10M trainable parameters and ~18M frozen-at-random-init parameters.

Custom tokenizer = full model capacity goes into the parts that learn.

(For ~1B+ models trained on diverse corpora, this reverses — pretrained tokenizers become fine because the embedding rows are a small fraction of total parameters and most of them will see real gradient updates.)

### `torch.compile` is free 1.5–2× on H100

One line: `model = torch.compile(model)` after `.to(DEVICE)`. First training step takes 60s for graph tracing; subsequent steps are ~1.5–2× faster than eager mode. No code changes elsewhere, no quality impact.

Combined with `bf16` autocast (`with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):`) the throughput improvement on H100 SXM is wild: my 50K-step run sustained ~1.4M tokens/sec end-to-end. That's how 50K steps fit in 20 minutes.

### TensorBoard scalars are basically free, and they replace guesswork

Adding ~10 lines (`SummaryWriter(log_dir=...)` + `writer.add_scalar(...)` calls) gave me train/loss, train/lr, val/loss, val/perplexity, perf/tokens-per-sec graphed over training. Negligible runtime cost. After the run, I could see in 10 seconds whether the model converged, when warmup ended, whether throughput was steady or degrading.

You can't reason about whether a model is "done training" without seeing the curve. Always log scalars.

## What I'd do differently next time

1. **Pull `data_cache/` off the first pod and reuse on subsequent pods.** Saves 5–10 min of redownload + retokenisation per new pod. Just `scp -r` the directory to my laptop, then back up to the new pod when I provision one.

2. **Use `cd /workspace && git clone`** on the pod, not `cd / && git clone`. RunPod mounts the persistent network volume at `/workspace`; cloning to `/` puts everything on container disk that vanishes on stop.

3. **Set spending alerts** on day 0. RunPod → Account → Billing → Spending Limits. Cap monthly at a number I'm willing to lose if I leave a pod running by accident.

4. **Always `set -euo pipefail` in shell scripts.** Caught one bug-in-progress because of it (the script kept executing past a failed `scp`, which would have left me with a half-pulled checkpoint).

5. **Train_d12 first, train_d8 only as a smoke test.** The d8 model never produced anything I'd want to share. Should have run it for ~5 min as a "does the pipeline work" check, then moved straight to d12.

## Where to go from here

To produce a model that actually *knows things* (not just stories), the natural next experiment:

- **Bigger corpus**: FineWeb-edu, ~1B token sample. Real-world knowledge encoded.
- **Same model size or slightly larger**: 91M is enough capacity for general English at a small scale.
- **Cost**: ~$15–20 of compute, ~6 hours wall-clock on a single H100.

Or to get a real chatbot:

- **Karpathy's nanochat speedrun**: pretraining + mid-training + SFT + (optional) RLHF.
- **Hardware**: 8× H100 for ~3 hours.
- **Cost**: ~$100.
- **Result**: a 561M-param model that can hold a conversation and do simple math.

I won't do either tonight, but they're both well within hobby-budget territory. That's the headline.

## Things I didn't expect to learn

1. **The model is mostly its tokenizer at small scale.** ~65% of "params" can be wasted vocabulary embeddings if you pick the tokenizer wrong.

2. **Loss curves are honest.** If you can read them, you know exactly when to stop training. No guesswork required.

3. **MPS isn't a real GPU yet.** Two distinct Apple-Silicon-related bugs in one weekend. Useful for laptop development, frustrating for real inference.

4. **Sampling matters as much as training.** A well-trained model with bad sampling settings (no repetition penalty, wide top-k, low temperature) looks like a bad model. Three slider tweaks turned my d8 from "broken" to "OK".

5. **Cost scales sub-linearly with quality at this size.** $0.30 → $1.50 = 5× cost for what feels like 50× quality jump (gibberish → coherent stories). The marginal returns to scale are wild at the very small end.

6. **Tooling matters more than algorithms.** TensorBoard, tmux, scp scripts, the inference UI — half my effort, all the difference between "I sort of trained a model" and "I trained a model and can prove it."

7. **My dog might be famous now.** Unclear how to feel about this.

## What this whole thing actually proves

Three years ago "training your own LLM" was a corporate research lab activity that took millions of dollars and a quarter of engineering time. Today it's a **Saturday-afternoon hobby project at the cost of a pizza**.

The architectural ideas are public. The training stack is open-source. The compute is rentable by the second. The corpora are downloadable. There is nothing left in the way of someone who wants to learn how language models actually work *by training one*. You can read every line of code that runs. You can change every line of code that runs. You can train, evaluate, deploy, and inspect a working transformer over a weekend, with your own custom tokenizer and your own choice of corpus.

The cost barrier is essentially gone for the educational version. The frontier-scale version is still expensive, but the educational version is the one most engineers should actually do — because the *shape* of the work is the same. You don't need to train GPT-4 to understand how GPT-4 works. You need to train *something*, see the loss curve, watch the gibberish slowly become English, debug the inevitable NaN, and feel the moment when a coherent story drops out of a system you built from scratch.

And once you've done it, you can go back to using big models with both a much better intuition for what they're doing internally and the cheerful realisation that the magic is, well, just a lot of matmul and a careful training loop.
